## 1. What this round is actually testing

The manager is from a tech background. That changes how this round behaves vs. an HR screen:

- **HR filtered for fit, eligibility, communication.** Manager filters for: *"Would I want this person on my engagement team next month?"*
- **They will probe technical depth inside behavioral answers.** When you say "I built a Power BI dashboard," they may ask "what schema, what filtered the noise, how did stakeholders consume it." Don't be caught flat — your stories must hold up under one or two technical follow-ups.
- **They are listening for STAR discipline.** If you ramble, they assume you'll ramble in front of clients. Tight 90-second to 2-minute STAR answers are the standard.
- **They are scoring your judgement, not just your activity.** What did you decide? Why? What would you do differently?
- **They want to see a future colleague.** Be conversational, ask clarifying questions, don't perform.

**Likely format:** 45–60 minutes. ~5 min intros + their context, ~35–45 min behavioral Q&A, ~5–10 min your questions, ~2 min wrap.

## 4. Top 20 behavioral questions you should be ready for

Each question gets a story pointer + a 1-line *EY-flavored hook* — the angle that lands best for a TST manager.

### Block A — Delivery, ownership, ambiguity (most likely to be asked)

**Q1. "Tell me about a time you delivered results despite competing priorities or ambiguity."**
→ **Story 1 (20 Pilots)**
*Hook:* The framework I built is the same kind I'd use on an EY engagement: prioritize by strategic alignment + feasibility + impact, communicate cadence by tier, kill what isn't delivering.

**Q2. "Tell me about a time you took ownership of something outside your formal scope."**
→ **Story 5 (Ford Latency)** or **Story 10 (Haptic Feedback)**
*Hook:* In consulting nobody hands you the right ticket — you see it, you own it, you bring people along.

**Q3. "Describe a project where you had to make a recommendation with incomplete information."**
→ **Story 8 (Valet Parking)**
*Hook:* In TST work, you'll rarely have full visibility into a client's legacy estate. The discipline is honest scenario framing, explicit risk-return, and durable analysis that pays off later.

**Q4. "Tell me about a time you missed a deadline or a project didn't go as planned."**
→ **Story 8 (Valet Parking — didn't get approved)** or a softer angle: **Story 6 (IIKB — original design wasn't right)**
*Hook:* Don't pretend you've never had a project not land. The judgement is what you took from it.

### Block B — Stakeholder management, influence, conflict

**Q5. "Tell me about a time you had to influence someone without authority."**
→ **Story 7 (PoC Business Model, Mobis)**
*Hook:* Reframe, don't argue. Each stakeholder needs their own reason to say yes.

**Q6. "Describe a difficult conversation you had with a client or stakeholder."**
→ **Story 3 (Partner Rescue, Mobis)**
*Hook:* Lead with what's working, name the gap explicitly, give them a revised plan they can trust.

**Q7. "Tell me about a time you disagreed with a manager or senior leader."**
→ **Story 6 (IIKB Criticism, NAV)** but flip the framing — *I disagreed initially, then realized she was right strategically and proposed a phased compromise.*
*Hook:* Disagreement is data, not a fight. The win is finding the third option neither side started with.

**Q8. "Tell me about earning trust with a skeptical stakeholder."**
→ **Story 2 (NAV Credibility)** or **Story 7 (PoC Business Model)**
*Hook:* Trust is quick wins + transparency about what you don't know + obsessive follow-through.

**Q9. "Tell me about navigating a matrixed organization."**
→ **Story 5 (Ford Latency)** or **Story 7 (PoC Business Model)**
*Hook:* Matrix orgs reward people who design coalitions, not people who argue louder.

### Block C — Learning, adaptability, growth

**Q10. "Describe a time you had to learn a new domain or skill quickly."**
→ **Story 11 (Agentic AI Briefing)**
*Hook:* Knowledge map first, structured sources second, learning alongside the audience third.

**Q11. "Tell me about feedback that changed how you work."**
→ **Story 6 (IIKB)**
*Hook:* My instinct toward simplicity was right technically, but her instinct about the business problem was right strategically. Both can be true.

**Q12. "What's a weakness you're actively working on?"**
→ Real one: *"My production software engineering experience is narrower than my strategy background — I've been deliberately closing that gap with hands-on JavaScript and Java work using Copilot, Claude Code, and MCP servers over the last twelve months. It's a closing gap, not a closed one."*

### Block D — Leadership, team, coaching

**Q13. "Tell me about leading a team."**
→ **Story 1 (20 Pilots — managed 2–3 analysts)** or **Story A (PnP COVID — held the team and onboarded juniors)**
*Hook:* The JD asks for "managing teams of 2–3 resources" — anchor exactly there.

**Q14. "Tell me about coaching or developing someone."**
→ Sub-component of **Story A (PnP COVID — onboarded the new juniors against year-long contracts)** or **Story 10 (Haptic — coached the startup founder on go-to-market)**

**Q15. "Tell me about a time you had to deliver tough feedback."**
→ Reverse of Story 6 — a moment you had to be the one giving feedback. Have one ready (e.g., redirecting a junior analyst's research framing at PnP).

### Block E — Innovation, judgment, strategic thinking

**Q16. "Tell me about a strategic recommendation you made to leadership."**
→ **Story 11 (Agentic AI)** or **Story 5 (NAV OI briefing — separate from the AI one)**
*Hook:* Make it actionable, not theoretical. Every gap mapped to a specific fix.

**Q17. "Tell me about a creative or unconventional solution you came up with."**
→ **Story 10 (Haptic Feedback)** — cross-pollination from gaming to automotive
*Hook:* Best innovation comes from outside the industry. AI-for-modernization is the same pattern.

**Q18. "Tell me about a data-driven decision you made."**
→ **Story 4 (Pipeline Dashboards)** or **Story 5 (Ford Latency — Splunk + Python correlation)**

### Block F — Why EY / why consulting (manager-flavored)

**Q19. "Why consulting?"**
→ Use the version-A answer from `hr_screen_prep.md` §3, but lean harder on the *manager* angle: *"Consulting is structurally about pattern-recognition across many client engagements. After 7 years of running quasi-consulting work inside companies, this is the role where I get to compound that experience instead of restarting it."*

**Q20. "Why EY TST specifically, why now, why this team?"**
→ Three beats:
1. The role itself — TST's combination of strategy + transformation + AI is an unusually well-defined intersection. Most firms split those.
2. Federal practice + your NAV CANADA + clearance eligibility lines up directly.
3. *"What I've seen of how EY adopts frontier AI tooling — MCP, Copilot, agentic workflows — tells me consultants here actually build with the tools, not just brief on them. That matters to me because the skill I'm bringing is hands-on."*

## 5. Tech-flavored behavioral questions — high probability given a tech-background manager

A tech manager will smuggle in questions that look behavioral but probe technical judgment. Be ready.

**T1. "Tell me about a time you assessed a legacy system."**
→ **Story 6 (IIKB Power Platform)** or angle Story 5 differently — Ford connected vehicle service was effectively a legacy assessment.
*Manager probe to expect:* *"What did you actually look at first?"* — Have an answer: dependency map, entry points, data flows, third-party libraries, security posture, recent change history.

**T2. "How do you simplify a technical concept for a non-technical client?"**
→ **Story 11 (Agentic AI)**
*Hook:* I don't dumb it down — I anchor on what the technology *does for them*, then introduce mechanism only as far as needed. *"Agentic AI = an LLM that plans multi-step tasks and uses tools, not just answers questions"* — translated into "this can autonomously audit a legacy codebase for SQL injection without a human reading every line."

**T3. "Tell me about a project where you used AI or emerging technology."**
→ **Story 11 (Agentic AI Briefing)** is your hero story. Secondary: discuss your hands-on MCP server and Copilot/Claude Code work since the co-op ended.
*Manager probe to expect:* *"What MCP server did you actually set up?"* — Have a concrete answer ready: filesystem MCP, GitHub MCP, weather quickstart, custom server you authored, etc.

**T4. "Walk me through how you'd approach assessing a client's legacy app for cyber risk using AI."**
→ Not a STAR question — a *judgement* question. Walk them through:
1. Discovery — ingest codebase via filesystem MCP + dependency map
2. Static analysis — run SAST tooling, layer LLM triage on top of findings to filter false positives and explain exploitability
3. Cross-cut with CVE database for outdated dependencies (Log4Shell-style)
4. Targeted LLM passes against OWASP Top 10 patterns: injection, hardcoded secrets, weak crypto, deserialization, broken access control
5. Output: risk register with severity + exploitability + remediation path, not just a vulnerability dump
6. Position AI as triage + explanation + remediation drafting, *not* the source of truth — verify with SAST/DAST

**T5. "How do you know when to trust an AI-generated output?"**
→ Three filters:
1. *Can I explain it?* — never accept a suggestion you can't justify line-by-line
2. *Does it have tests?* — characterization tests catch silent regressions
3. *What's the blast radius if it's wrong?* — security findings get verified, scaffolding can be looser

**T6. "Tell me about a technical problem where the obvious solution was wrong."**
→ **Story 5 (Ford Latency)** — operations called it "normal variability"; correlation analysis proved it was deployment-correlated load balancer misconfig.

**T7. "How do you balance technical debt vs. delivery velocity?"**
→ Use **Story 6 (IIKB)** as a frame: my instinct was the simpler design (less debt); manager's instinct was the more controlled design (more upfront work). The phased rollout was the right answer. Generalize: *I look for the smallest reversible step that buys real learning.*

**T8. "How do you handle a situation where the client wants something you think is the wrong technical decision?"**
→ Three-step approach: (1) make sure I understand *why* they want it — usually there's a real constraint I haven't seen, (2) name my concern with specific evidence not opinion, (3) propose an option that addresses their underlying need without the technical risk. Then disagree-and-commit.

## 6. Trickier questions you should not be caught flat on

**X1. "What's a project you'd do differently in hindsight?"**
→ **Story 8 (Valet Parking)** — frame the lesson: *I would have invested earlier in a smaller, lower-risk PoC scoping conversation with leadership before doing the full evaluation, so the work-to-decision ratio was tighter.*

**X2. "Tell me about a time you were wrong."**
→ **Story 6 (IIKB)** — my initial design was wrong because I solved the wrong problem.

**X3. "Tell me about a conflict on a team."**
→ **Story 7 (R&D resistance at Mobis)** — but frame it as a productive conflict: their pushback was legitimate and the redesign was better for it.

**X4. "How do you handle stress?"**
→ Don't claim you don't feel stress — that reads inauthentic. Real answer: *"I get sharper under pressure when the problem is well-defined; I get diffuse when the problem is ambiguous. So I invest the first hour of any high-stress situation in defining what we're actually solving for, who the decision-makers are, and what 'done' looks like. After that, the execution is calm."*

**X5. "What kind of consultant do you want to be in 5 years?"**
→ *"The person a client calls when they have a modernization-plus-AI problem and don't know whether to lead with strategy or execution — because I can credibly do both. Within EY, that probably looks like a senior consultant or manager track in TST, owning engagement teams, contributing to how the practice itself adopts AI tooling."*

**X6. "What questions do you have about my background or this team?"**
→ Have one: *"I'd love to hear how you got into the TST practice from a tech background — and what you wish you'd known in your first year."* Mirrors them, opens conversation, signals interest.

**X7. "Is there anything you'd like to ask that you haven't gotten the chance to?"**
→ The *gracious recovery* question. If you flubbed something earlier, this is your chance: *"Earlier when you asked about [topic], I gave a partial answer — I'd like to add [the part you missed]."* Senior interviewers respect this.

**X8. "Why should we hire you over someone with a CS degree and 5 years of pure dev experience?"**
→ Direct, confident, no apology: *"Because the role is consulting, not pure development. You can find a stronger pure dev — but a stronger combination of seven years of strategy delivery, tech consulting in regulated environments, hands-on AI tooling, and federal-clearance-eligible client experience is harder to find. I'd rather be the consultant who can build alongside engineers than the engineer trying to learn consulting."*

## 7. Resume-specific questions the manager will probe (different from HR)

HR checked claims. The manager will go deeper.

**R1. "Walk me through the NAV CANADA Agentic AI briefing — what was actually in the deck?"**
Be ready: structure was (a) what Agentic AI is and what's changed in the last 12 months, (b) where it applies in air navigation context, (c) vendor/tooling landscape, (d) recommended investment posture. Mention you used Claude/Copilot/MCP setups in the research itself, not just *about* them.

**R2. "The PoC business model story — who was the hardest stakeholder to convince and why?"**
→ R&D leadership. Reason: they own production quality, so any miss in a startup PoC reflects on them. The reframe was making them service-providers to external partners, which gave them upside (early tech access) without quality liability.

**R3. "Tell me more about the Ford latency fix — what made you suspect a load balancer specifically?"**
→ Splunk showed spikes were *clustered* in time windows, not random. Correlation with deployments + the fact that one specific service was always involved suggested infrastructure config rather than application logic. Walking through that diagnostic logic shows the manager you actually did the work.

**R4. "Your resume mentions Power BI dashboards multiple times — what's a dashboard you built that was *not* used?"**
→ Tricky one. Be honest: an early Plug and Play prototype that tracked engagement events at a granularity nobody actually consumed. Lesson: I now design dashboards starting from the decision they're meant to support, not the data I happen to have.

**R5. "You moved from automotive to aviation/federal at NAV CANADA — what was hardest?"**
→ Vocabulary, regulatory tempo, and the absence of consumer pressure as a forcing function. Adapted by interviewing internal SMEs in week one and treating myself as someone learning alongside the audience.

**R6. "What's a technology trend you're watching right now that we should be paying attention to in TST?"**
→ Pick something concrete you actually have a take on. Suggested: *"Computer-use / agentic browser agents — they're at the inflection where agents can drive UIs that aren't API-accessible. For modernization that's significant, because a lot of legacy admin functions only exist as UI flows, and we suddenly have a path to wrap them programmatically without a rebuild."*

## 8. Questions to ask the manager (pick 3–4)

Tech-managers are bored by generic questions. Mix one personal/career question with technical/team questions.

1. **"How did you end up in EY TST from a tech background, and what did you wish you'd known in your first year?"**
2. **"What's the engagement mix on your team right now — federal vs. private, modernization vs. transaction support, AI-led vs. traditional?"**
3. **"When you're staffing a modernization engagement, what's the ideal team shape — backgrounds, roles, ratios?"**
4. **"What's the gap between how clients *describe* their AI ambitions and what they're actually ready to operationalize, and how does the team handle that gap?"**
5. **"Where in the TST stack is the firm investing the most in tooling — Copilot, MCP, agentic frameworks, or something else?"**
6. **"What does success look like at the 6-month and 1-year mark for someone in this role?"**
7. **"What's the next round in the process, and what would be most useful for me to prepare for it?"**

## 9. Day-of approach

**Hour-of warmup (in order):**
1. Re-read this doc + the **STAR Stories Bank** in Notion (don't cram new material)
2. Re-read the JD aloud
3. Re-read your EY resume aloud
4. Speak the opening + Story 1 + Story 5 + Story 11 out loud once each
5. Glass of water, no notifications, log in 10 min early

**During the interview:**
- Pause before answering. 5-10 seconds of silence beats 90 seconds of rambling.
- If you don't have a perfect story, say so: *"I don't have a perfect example for that exact situation, but here's a closely related experience — let me know if it doesn't quite hit what you were after."*
- Use "I" not "we." This is your story, not your team's.
- Land every answer on the **Result** with something quantified or concrete.
- Match their energy — if they're warm, be warm; if they're crisp, be crisp.

**The structural rule:**
> 30 seconds of *Situation* + *Task*, 60-75 seconds of *Action*, 15-20 seconds of *Result*. Total ~2 minutes. If you're past 2:30, you're rambling.

## 10. The single most important framing

**This manager is going to spend an hour deciding whether you'd be a credible voice in front of their clients.** Every answer should leave them thinking *"I could put her in front of a CIO next month."*

That means:
- **Specifics over generalities.** Tools you used, decisions you made, numbers when you have them.
- **Judgement over activity.** Not "what I did" but "why I decided that and what I learned."
- **Calm over performance.** Conversational beats rehearsed every time.
- **Forward-looking close.** When asked about the past, finish with what it means for how you'd work *now*.

You've already done the hard part — the 11-story bank from your Rogers and Amazon prep is more material than most candidates ever build. This round is about delivering it tightly under a tech manager's lens.

Good luck.
