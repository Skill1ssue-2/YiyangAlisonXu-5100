# EY TST Interview — Common Questions in STAR Format

## How to use this document

- **STAR** = Situation, Task, Action, Result. Every behavioral answer should have all four parts, in that order.
- **Target length**: 90 seconds to 2 minutes per answer. Practice out loud.
- **Rule**: Lead with one sentence of context, then spend 60% of your time on the *Action* (that's what they're scoring).
- **Always close on Result** — preferably quantified.
- Each answer below is mapped to the job competency it proves.

---

## Question 1 — "Tell me about a time you delivered under pressure."
**Competency tested:** Deliver Results, managing ambiguity, composure under pressure.
**Story:** Plug and Play COVID staffing crisis.

**Situation.** I was a Senior Venture Analyst at Plug and Play China leading innovation consulting for global automotive OEM and Tier 1 clients. Partway through the year, COVID hit and two senior analysts on my team quit within a short window. We had year-long signed client contracts — there was no backup, no documented process, and no obvious path to deliver.

**Task.** I needed to keep multiple concurrent client engagements on track, stabilize the team, and preserve the client relationships — all without simply telling the clients we couldn't deliver.

**Action.** I took three steps in parallel. First, I took full ownership of the delivery myself and built standardized frameworks for our two core activities — startup scouting and client events — so the work became repeatable instead of dependent on individual analyst knowledge. Second, I worked with HR to urgently hire and onboard junior analysts, and I ruthlessly prioritized the client work by impact so the most important deliverables never slipped. Third, I shadowed every client meeting I could to make sure communication stayed consistent and the clients saw a steady face even as the team churned underneath.

**Result.** Our biggest client engagement that year landed on time with no quality regression. That client signed a larger follow-on contract the next year, which was the team's biggest deal of the cycle. The scouting and event frameworks I built became the team standard and were still in use after I left.

**Why this fits EY TST:** It demonstrates delivery discipline, team leadership, client ownership, and the ability to systematize — all directly relevant to consulting engagement delivery.

## Question 2 — "Tell me about presenting to senior leadership."
**Competency tested:** Executive communication, strategic thinking, client-facing delivery.
**Story:** NAV CANADA Open Innovation briefing.

**Situation.** At NAV CANADA, senior leadership had been told to "do innovation" but nobody had defined what that actually meant operationally. The team was doing ad-hoc reactive work without a frame.

**Task.** I was asked to deliver a research briefing that would give senior leadership a concrete view of how open innovation should work at NAV CANADA — something they could act on, not another abstract strategy deck.

**Action.** I built the briefing around a structure I knew leadership could use: the org structure needed to support innovation, running from strategy through to a development pipeline. I mapped four collaboration modes with real case studies from Volvo, BMW, and Mobis M.START so the frame was grounded in industry precedent. I then did a current-state versus future-state gap analysis presented side-by-side, and critically, every identified gap was paired with a specific actionable fix — nothing aspirational, everything tied back to a concrete next step.

**Result.** Leadership used the briefing to shape their innovation investment priorities for the year. They told me it was the most concrete innovation presentation they had seen, and the format became the template for subsequent strategy briefings in the group.

**Why this fits EY TST:** EY TST is built on presenting strategy to senior clients and making it actionable. This is exactly that.

## Question 3 — "Tell me about a time you had to learn something new quickly."
**Competency tested:** Learning agility, bias for action, curiosity.
**Story:** NAV CANADA Agentic AI briefing.

**Situation.** At NAV CANADA, I was asked to produce an executive briefing on Agentic AI and its implications for air navigation modernization. I had no background in aviation, and at that point my own knowledge of Agentic AI was early — the field was moving fast. The deadline was three weeks.

**Task.** Deliver an executive-ready briefing with enough depth to influence investment priorities, on a topic I was still building fluency in, to an audience who would notice if I was faking it.

**Action.** I treated the three weeks as structured learning plus synthesis. Week one, I built a knowledge map — primary sources from AI research papers, secondary analysis from respected industry writers, and a short list of vendor offerings I could anchor against. Week two, I interviewed internal stakeholders — pilots, controllers, IT architects — to understand the aviation context where agentic systems might actually fit. Week three, I built the briefing around the framing "here is what this technology is, here is where it applies to our operations, here is what we'd need to invest in to adopt it responsibly." I positioned myself to the audience as someone learning alongside them, which kept the tone honest.

**Result.** The briefing landed on time. Senior leadership used it to shape the AI investment priorities for the group, and feedback was that it was the clearest explanation of Agentic AI they had received. The concrete outcome was that it directly fed into how the team was resourced for AI work in the following cycle.

**Why this fits EY TST:** Consulting constantly demands fast ramp into unfamiliar client domains. This proves the learning muscle is there and is paired with delivery discipline.

## Question 4 — "Tell me about managing a team."
**Competency tested:** Team leadership, delivery through others.
**Story:** Hyundai Mobis Ventures — 2–3 analyst delivery team on PoC feasibility studies.

**Situation.** At Hyundai Mobis Ventures in Shenzhen, I led the regional open innovation program, which meant running proof-of-concept evaluations across more than 200 global technology vendors, startups, and accelerators. The delivery engine was a rotating team of two to three analysts supporting the PoC pipeline.

**Task.** Direct the team to evaluate 20-plus PoC initiatives per cycle across connected vehicles, autonomous driving, and smart mobility — while maintaining quality high enough that Mobis R&D would trust the outputs as input to real investment decisions.

**Action.** I set up the team around three practices. First, I wrote playbooks for the recurring work — startup evaluation, competitive benchmarking, feasibility scoring — so a new analyst could produce a defensible first draft without me needing to handhold every step. Second, I assigned ownership of specific PoC workstreams to individual analysts so they had end-to-end accountability, but I stayed close on the executive-level synthesis myself because that's where the credibility of the program lived. Third, I instituted a weekly working session where the team brought in-progress work for peer review, which caught errors early and compounded skill across the group.

**Result.** The team consistently delivered the feasibility pipeline on schedule. Several PoCs moved into production partnerships, including one where a small Chinese haptic startup won a mass-production bid with Mobis — directly traceable to the team's scouting and evaluation work. The playbooks and scoring frameworks continued to be used after I left.

**Why this fits EY TST:** JD explicitly asks for "experience managing teams of 2–3 resources for project delivery."

## Question 5 — "Tell me about a difficult client or stakeholder."
**Competency tested:** Cross-functional influence, reframing conflict, earning trust.
**Story:** Mobis R&D rejecting startup PoCs.

**Situation.** At Hyundai Mobis, the R&D organization was pushing back on nearly every startup PoC I proposed. Their position was that regional startups couldn't outperform established Tier 1 vendors — and it was a legitimate concern, because R&D owns production quality and their credibility is on the line if something fails in the field.

**Task.** I needed R&D buy-in for the open innovation program to function at all, but I also couldn't just override their concerns because they were substantively valid. Arguing harder for the same position wasn't going to work.

**Action.** I changed the frame entirely. Instead of pitching R&D as a "gatekeeper" that should approve startups, I redesigned the collaboration model to position R&D as a service provider to external OEM innovation partners — meaning startups were a feed for partnerships, not a threat to quality standards. Then I worked through the five stakeholder groups involved — R&D, sales, legal, marketing, and executive sponsors — and built a specific value case for each. R&D got early access to emerging technology; sales got new OEM relationship leverage; legal got a standardized IP protection template; marketing got an innovation narrative. I took that bundle back to R&D leadership.

**Result.** R&D signed off, and the collaboration model became a repeatable business process across the open innovation program. The structural change worked because each stakeholder's concern was addressed in the design, not argued down. The specific haptic startup engagement that had been stalled moved forward and eventually won a mass-production bid.

**Why this fits EY TST:** Consulting work lives or dies on stakeholder alignment. This story shows the ability to diagnose resistance and redesign the engagement so it resolves — which is the core TST move.

## Question 6 — "Tell me about a time you received critical feedback."
**Competency tested:** Self-awareness, coachability, ego management.
**Story:** NAV CANADA IIKB (Innovation Knowledge Base) security groups.

**Situation.** At NAV CANADA, I was building a Power Platform knowledge management system for the innovation team. I had designed a clean, simple security model that I was pleased with. My manager pushed back hard and asked for a much more granular security group customization.

**Task.** Respond to the feedback well — meaning, figure out whether she was right, whether I was right, or whether there was a better answer than either of our starting positions.

**Action.** My first instinct was defensive — I'd already put work into a design I thought was elegant and I wanted to defend it. I caught myself doing that, stepped back, and actually listened to what she was concerned about. Her worry was legitimate: if everyone had equal access, half-baked ideas would reach leadership and dilute the signal. That wasn't the problem I had been solving for, but it was a real problem. So instead of either her fully granular model or my fully open model, I proposed a phased rollout with two or three tiers, observing real user behavior before deciding whether more granularity was needed.

**Result.** The phased approach turned out to be better than either starting position. We learned that some gatekeeping did improve quality — so her instinct was directionally right. But we also learned that a couple of the tiers she had originally wanted were never exercised in practice, so the full granular design would have been over-engineered. The system shipped with the right amount of structure for the first year of adoption.

**Why this fits EY TST:** Consultants get corrected by partners and clients constantly. Handling it without ego is foundational.

## Question 7 — "Tell me about a time you took initiative / went beyond your role."
**Competency tested:** Ownership, bias for action, customer obsession.
**Story:** Haptic feedback matchmaking at Mobis.

**Situation.** At Mobis, I met a small Chinese haptic feedback software company at a pitch event. Their gaming technology was at the level of the PlayStation 5 DualSense controller — genuinely strong — but they had zero automotive industry experience and no automotive clients. Nobody had asked me to do anything with them; they weren't part of any active PoC pipeline.

**Task.** I didn't have to do anything. But I saw a real opportunity and decided to invest time in it.

**Action.** I talked with the founder in Mandarin — the cultural and business language fluency mattered because they weren't used to working with international OEM-style players. I identified two concrete automotive use cases where their technology could plug in: lane departure warning haptics, and a 3D touch infotainment screen. I coached them on go-to-market strategy — specifically, demo the concept first using their gaming portfolio rather than trying to build a full automotive-grade solution from a cold start. Then I connected them with a seat manufacturer and with Mobis's infotainment group as the hardware packagers, so the startup could focus on their algorithms rather than hardware productization.

**Result.** Their technology ended up being demoed at auto shows and at Mobis's global R&D showcase. They won a mass-production bid — taking a startup with zero automotive presence to a clear commercial path. For Mobis, the program got a visible innovation win that made it easier to justify the open innovation budget internally.

**Why this fits EY TST:** Demonstrates the client-obsession and initiative mindset EY wants — creating value beyond the narrow scope of the ask.

## Question 8 — "Tell me about a data-driven decision you made."
**Competency tested:** Analytical mindset, data fluency.
**Story:** Plug and Play startup research database.

**Situation.** At Plug and Play, critical startup information lived in individual analysts' heads — contact lists were personal property, and market research was scattered across spreadsheets and emails. Every client engagement required rebuilding context from scratch, and when analysts left the team, their knowledge left with them.

**Task.** Build a system that would make the team's research capability durable, data-driven, and independent of any individual's memory.

**Action.** I partnered with IT to build a SQL database with a Power BI front end covering more than 200 startups. The data model captured not just company metadata but engagement history, technology tags, and PoC outcomes. I designed data entry to be low-friction so analysts would actually keep it current — because adoption dies if updates feel like overhead. I built three dashboards on top: engagement activity tracking, technology-trend distribution, and geographic coverage. And I documented the whole thing so it would survive independent of me.

**Result.** The database became the primary tool used for every client delivery on the team. It eliminated the misalignment we used to get when two analysts had different views of the same startup, and it continued running after I left the company. In quantitative terms it supported scouting and analysis work across the entire client book.

**Why this fits EY TST:** Systematizing knowledge so the organization can scale delivery is core to consulting value — and directly transferable to how AI + MCP + knowledge bases will be used in modernization work.

## Question 9 — "Tell me about a time your work wasn't used or a project failed."
**Competency tested:** Resilience, long-term thinking, non-defensive framing.
**Story:** Autonomous valet parking evaluation.

**Situation.** At Mobis, I was asked to evaluate indoor autonomous valet parking technology — specifically to assess every candidate communication technology (WiFi, BLE, 5G, UWB) and review every supplier in the space. I did a deep technical and commercial evaluation.

**Task.** Produce a recommendation on whether Mobis should invest in the space, and if so, in which technology stack and which partner.

**Action.** I ran a structured comparative evaluation across the communication technologies, visited suppliers, built a phased-deployment proposal, and flagged every uncertainty I could see. I presented a range of scenarios rather than a single prescriptive answer, because the technology readiness genuinely varied by environment.

**Result.** The project did not get approved in that cycle — the investment committee decided timing wasn't right for Mobis.

**The real result came a year later.** An automatic valet parking proof-of-concept was greenlit in a different business unit, and the sensor evaluation and vendor assessments I had documented became directly usable input for that PoC. The analysis wasn't wasted — it was just used later than intended. The lesson I took from it is that rigorous analysis always has downstream value as long as you document it well, and I've carried that discipline forward.

**Why this fits EY TST:** Shows maturity about project outcomes. In consulting not every recommendation is adopted — what matters is that the work is durable and the practitioner doesn't get demoralized.

## Question 10 — "Tell me about a time you solved a hard technical problem."
**Competency tested:** Technical depth, ownership, dive-deep mindset.
**Story:** Ford connected vehicle latency fix.

**Situation.** At Ford, one of the connected vehicle services I supported had intermittent latency spikes. The operations team had classified them as "normal variability" and moved on. Customer complaints about the feature were increasing in parallel.

**Task.** Nobody had explicitly asked me to investigate — it wasn't formally my ticket — but the pattern of complaints was worrying and the existing explanation didn't satisfy me.

**Action.** I pulled Splunk data covering a few weeks of the service's behavior and ran Python analysis to correlate the latency spikes with other events in the environment. I found the spikes lined up with specific deployments. I traced the root cause to a misconfigured load balancer that was behaving badly under certain release conditions. I then brought product, engineering, and QA together to align on a fix, and I pushed for a monitoring check to be added so future similar issues would surface immediately instead of living silently.

**Result.** Latency dropped roughly 30% after the fix. Customer complaints on the feature dropped correspondingly. The leadership team cited the work as an example of the kind of ownership they wanted to see on the broader platform, and the monitoring check I pushed in caught two additional similar issues in subsequent release cycles.

**Why this fits EY TST:** Shows technical depth, proactive ownership, and the ability to drive cross-functional fixes — all relevant to legacy modernization engagements where the hard work is finding root causes in unfamiliar systems.

## Question 12 — "What are your weaknesses?"
**Not a STAR question — but must be handled well.**

**Good answer pattern:** Real weakness + real fix in motion + no fake humility.

**Option A (recommended):**
*"I over-invest in the framing and structure of a deliverable early, sometimes at the expense of getting a rough draft in front of stakeholders sooner. I've gotten better at this by forcing myself to share a working outline within 48 hours of starting a new piece of work, before I've worked out all the details — it gets me feedback earlier and the final product is usually better for it. I still have to actively resist the perfectionism instinct, but the delta is real."*

**Option B (if you want to lean into tech):**
*"My hands-on software engineering experience is narrower than my strategy and analysis experience — I've been a heavy user of Python for data work, but not a career application developer. Over the last year I've been deliberately pushing into AI-assisted JavaScript and Java development, MCP server authoring, and agentic workflows, because I want the consulting recommendations I make to be grounded in actually having shipped code, not just reviewed it. It's a closing gap, not a closed one, and I expect to keep pushing on it."*

## Rehearsal rules

1. Practice each answer out loud at least twice. Silent reading doesn't count.
2. Time yourself. Under 90 seconds is too thin; over 2 minutes is too long.
3. Record one full mock run and play it back. You'll hear filler words and fix them.
4. The night before, re-read this document once — don't cram.
5. During the interview, if you draw a blank, say: *"Let me think about that for a second."* Ten seconds of silence is fine. Do not fill it with rambling.
